PhD project · 2025–2028

Somatic mutation variation in plants

Plants have no segregated germline: a mutation arising in a growing shoot can be passed on through the flowers that shoot eventually bears. Yet compared with animals, remarkably little is known about how often somatic mutations occur in plants or what governs their rate.

With Alex Cagan (University of Cambridge) and the Wellcome Sanger Institute, we are measuring somatic mutation rates in long-lived versus short-lived plant species, and benchmarking sequencing technologies for how reliably they call low-frequency variants. This work is supported by a Cambridge Philosophical Society Sedgwick PhD studentship.

Genome structure

Plant organelle genomes

Plant mitochondrial genomes are among the strangest in biology: they recombine across repeats, exist as multiple alternative structures within one cell, and vary more than a hundred-fold in size. That makes them notoriously hard to assemble — and fascinating to compare.

Building on tools developed at the Sanger Institute (gfatk and Oatk), we are assembling mitochondrial genomes across a phylogenetically diverse sample of plants to ask how structure and gene order (synteny) evolve. A synthesis paper is in preparation with collaborators at EMBL-EBI and the Darwin Tree of Life.

Comparative genomics

Telomeres across the tree of life

Telomeres cap the ends of linear chromosomes with short tandem repeats — TTAGGG in vertebrates, TTTAGGG in most plants. But as reference genomes multiply, it is clear that canonical repeats vary far more than once thought, with some lineages having switched sequence entirely.

We developed the telomere identification toolkit (tidk) to discover and map these repeats rapidly, and are now applying it across the Darwin Tree of Life genomes to chart where and how often telomere sequences have changed.

Evolutionary ecology

Hybridisation & polyploidy

Hybridisation is a creative force in plant evolution, but it is far from evenly distributed: some genera hybridise constantly, others almost never. Using the whole British flora as a natural experiment, we showed that genetic factors — notably genome size and ploidy differences — predict where hybrids form.

We are particularly interested in crosses between plants of different ploidy, once assumed to be evolutionary dead ends but increasingly recognised as an important route for gene flow.

Field & greenhouse ecology

Host–parasite interactions in hemiparasites

Hemiparasitic plants such as eyebrights photosynthesise for themselves yet tap the roots of neighbours for water and nutrients. They tend to have far wider host ranges than full (holo)parasites, but this generalism has rarely been quantified or linked to fitness in wild populations.

Large common-garden experiments showed that host identity, rather than broad functional group, determines how well Euphrasia performs. We are now developing oxygraphis, a toolkit for analysing bipartite ecological networks, to map host–parasite links in wild British eyebright populations.

Darwin Tree of Life

Reference genomes for the British flora

The Darwin Tree of Life project aims to sequence every eukaryotic species in Britain and Ireland. As a Research Associate at the Royal Botanic Garden Edinburgh, Max sits on the project's plant sampling working group and maintains its progress dashboard.

The lab contributes to chromosome-level reference genomes for British plants — from crop wild relatives like apple to the giant, densely packed genome of European mistletoe (Viscum album), currently in preparation.
