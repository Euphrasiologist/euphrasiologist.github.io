Polyploid genomes, chromosome by chromosome

A polyploid carries several copies of every chromosome. Whether those copies still pair and swap freely (autopolyploid-like), or fall into sets that keep apart (allopolyploid-like), is written in how different they are. ploidyspec measures this from haplotype-resolved genome assemblies with k-mers, with no reference genome or alignment: how far apart the copies of each chromosome are, along their whole length; which chromosomes are old duplicates of each other; and where copies have fused or split into lineages. Two tests for subgenomes: duplicated blocks and subgenome-specific repeats

The two tests share no data. Across, the k-mer distance test: how much of the genome sits in blocks duplicated on another chromosome. Up, the repeat test: transposons that spread after two progenitors separated mark one subgenome and not the other, so k-mers found in some copies but not all should split the copies the same way again and again. The height is how much more often they do than with the copies shuffled, and the groups must also match the distance split to count. Allopolyploids should sit top right, diploids and autopolyploids bottom left; species off that diagonal are where the two disagree. Hollow rings are tests that could not be read cleanly. How to read this

Distance is not origin

How far apart two copies are tells you how long ago they stopped recombining, not how the polyploid formed. Autopolyploids that rediploidised long ago look allo-like, and rediploidisation can take tens of millions of years: in salmonids about a quarter of the genome rediploidised late, separately in each lineage, and sturgeons were still about half tetraploid 80 million years after their duplication (Lien et al. 2016; Redmond et al. 2023). And the divergence between subgenomes dates the split of their parent species, not the polyploidy (Dunn & Sethuraman 2025). So we say disomic-like and tetrasomic-like, and keep allo and auto for the literature.

Mixed genomes are normal

Chromosomes in one genome can differ: sturgeon, peanut and Brachiaria have tetrasomic and disomic chromosomes side by side, and pairing in tetraploid rose differs even between the arms of one chromosome (Dalle Palle et al. 2022; Leal-Bertioli et al. 2018; Bourke et al. 2017). That is why every reading here is per chromosome. Haplotype labels (HAP1–HAP4) are arbitrary per chromosome too: in these assemblies HAP1 of one chromosome need not belong with HAP1 of the next (Howe et al. 2026).

Transposon markers work one way

Subgenomes from different parents carry their own transposon families for millions of years, so a clear transposon split means separate ancestry. No split proves nothing: the parents may have been too close, or the polyploid too old, for the signal to survive (Session & Rokhsar 2023; Jia et al. 2022).

Check the assembly first

Exchange between homeologs piles up near chromosome ends, but a contig placed on the wrong chromosome leaves the same mark (Deb et al. 2023; Gordon et al. 2019). A copy assembled less completely than its siblings can look divergent. And k-mer (Mash) distance tracks sequence divergence only while copies are more than about 90% identical, so deep homeologs are compressed (Ondov et al. 2016).
